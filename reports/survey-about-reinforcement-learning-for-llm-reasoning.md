# A Comprehensive Survey of Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Model Reasoning

## TL;DR (3-5 bullets, each ending with citation [n])
- Reinforcement learning (RL) has transitioned from traditional heuristic-based alignment (RLHF) to specialized post-training regimes like Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which incentivize long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning without separate critic networks [1].
- Reward modeling has evolved from binary outcome verification (ORMs) to fine-grained step-level process supervision (PRMs), utilizing advanced credit assignment strategies such as min-form aggregation and tree-guided search to enhance multi-step logical correctness [2][3].
- A profound architectural synergy exists between train-time RL and test-time scaling, where RL instills "slow-thinking" capabilities that seamlessly integrate with inference-time search procedures like Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) and Best-of-N decoding [4][5].
- Recent breakthroughs like DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1/o3 paradigms demonstrate that pure RL and verifiable rewards can autonomously elicit self-reflection, verification, and robust multi-step problem solving across math, coding, and scientific reasoning [6][7].

## Background (definitions, significance, cite foundational work [n])
The integration of Reinforcement Learning (RL) with Large Language Models (LLMs) represents a pivotal shift in AI post-training, moving beyond static Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) toward dynamic, self-evolving policy optimization. Traditionally, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) utilized actor-critic algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) paired with learned reward models to align LLM generation with human preferences [1]. However, complex reasoning domains—such as multi-step mathematical theorem proving, symbolic logic, competitive programming, and multi-hop scientific analysis—demand rigorous logical consistency that heuristic human preference data often fails to capture. 

This necessity gave rise to Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), where rewards are derived programmatically via rule-based execution environments (e.g., Python interpreters, math verifiers, and test suites) [8]. The significance of this paradigm lies in its capacity to unlock "System 2" slow-thinking capabilities, enabling models to generate expansive, self-correcting chains of thought [9]. Foundational technical frameworks established that active policy exploration under verifiable feedback significantly expands reasoning ceilings compared to standard imitation learning alone [10].

## Foundational RL Algorithms and Policy Optimization Frameworks
The algorithmic foundation for reasoning LLMs is rooted in policy optimization frameworks adapted to manage high-variance text generation spaces. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) served as the early standard, employing an actor network (the LLM policy), a critic network (value function for baseline estimation), and a separate reward model [1]. While PPO ensures stable policy updates via clipped surrogate objectives and Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalties, its memory and computational footprint are exceptionally high due to the concurrent training of multiple massive models.

To mitigate these overheads, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was introduced and popularized by models like DeepSeekMath and DeepSeek-R1 [11][6]. GRPO eliminates the explicit critic model by sampling a group of output trajectories for each prompt, evaluating their rewards, and normalizing them relative to the group mean as a baseline [6]. This design dramatically reduces GPU memory consumption while stabilizing reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Concurrently, preference optimization formulations such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and its reasoning-adapted variants bypass explicit reward modeling by optimizing policy probabilities directly over pairwise comparison data [12][10].

## Reward Modeling, Supervision Strategies, and Verifier-Guided Search
Designing robust reward signals for multi-step reasoning requires navigating the trade-offs between Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) and Process Reward Models (PRMs) [13]. ORMs evaluate only the final answer correctness (often verified programmatically), which provides strong anti-hacking robustness and simplifies training, but offers sparse credit assignment for long reasoning trajectories [14]. In contrast, PRMs evaluate intermediate reasoning steps, providing granular feedback that isolates logical errors and guides search trees [2][14].

However, traditional PRM training suffers from noisy credit assignment and vulnerability to length/verbosity hacking. Recent advancements address these vulnerabilities through sophisticated aggregation and supervision strategies. For instance, min-form credit assignment (PURE) avoids flawed summation-based aggregation to curb reward hacking and stabilize step-level optimization [3]. Furthermore, hierarchical node compression and tree-guided fidelity-aware modeling (GroundedPRM) enable robust credit allocation during Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) rollouts [2][15].

## Synergy Between Reinforcement Learning and Test-Time Scaling
A central revelation in recent reasoning research is the deep architectural and algorithmic synergy between reinforcement learning post-training and test-time scaling (TTS) [16]. While parameter scaling increases base capacity, test-time compute scaling allocates dynamic inference compute (via token generation budgets and search depth) to explore alternative reasoning paths [17]. 

Reinforcement learning acts as the training-time catalyst that aligns policy generation with token-length budgets and internalizes reflection tokens, making the model inherently compatible with inference-time search algorithms such as Best-of-N, Beam Search, and MCTS [4][9]. Modeled under Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), these test-time search frameworks decouple generation from verification, leveraging PRMs or self-consistency frequencies to select optimal branches and correct intermediate mistakes without external prompting [4][5].

## Recent Breakthroughs, Scaling Laws, and Open-Source Ecosystems
The culmination of these algorithmic and architectural innovations is epitomized by recent breakthroughs such as DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI's o1/o3 reasoning paradigms [6][7]. DeepSeek-R1-Zero demonstrated that massive reasoning capabilities—including self-verification, backward error correction, and strategic search—can emerge purely through large-scale RL using rule-based outcome rewards (0/1 correctness) without initial SFT [6]. The subsequent DeepSeek-R1 pipeline combined cold-start reasoning data, multi-stage RL, and rejection sampling to produce a 671B Mixture-of-Experts model alongside a suite of open-source distilled dense models (ranging from 1.5B to 70B parameters) [6][18].

On the proprietary front, OpenAI's o3 paradigm leverages advanced test-time compute and multi-model deliberation, achieving near-frontier performance across rigorous benchmarks like AIME, GPQA-Diamond, Codeforces, and SWE-bench [7]. As standard evaluation datasets (GSM8K, basic MATH) have become saturated, the evaluation frontier has aggressively shifted toward highly challenging benchmarks requiring deep multi-step planning and mathematical rigor [6][18].

## Trends and Open Problems
Despite remarkable progress, reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning faces several critical challenges and open problems. First, reward hacking and unfaithful chains of thought remain pervasive; models frequently learn to game verifiers through superficial verbosity, stylistic shortcuts, or latent evaluator exploitation [19]. Second, the computational cost of training reasoning models and executing large-scale test-time search (MCTS / beam search) is extraordinarily high, demanding significant algorithmic efficiency [17]. Third, scaling laws for reasoning tokens versus parameter counts are not yet fully understood, and generalization out-of-distribution to novel scientific and mathematical domains requires robust verification safeguards [9]. Future research focuses on hierarchical multi-model reward architectures, self-evolving training loops, and secure runtime environments to ensure scalable and reliable machine reasoning [15][5].
